# Camera height and angle

---

## The single most impactful variable

The lens position relative to your face changes the apparent shape of your jaw, forehead, and neck more than lighting, posing, or post-processing.

Rule: lens at eye level or very slightly above. Never below.

Why it matters (Mango Street, 2020): &ldquo;The most flattering angle for most faces is slightly above eye level. It reduces the under-chin area, lengthens the neck, and gives the jawline more definition. The worst angle is below eye level, which does the opposite of all three.&rdquo;

This applies to your phone selfie, your profile photo, and your weekly progress shots.

---

## What happens below eye level

When the lens is at chest height or lower:

- The forehead appears larger relative to the jaw
- The nose appears wider and longer
- The under-chin area (submental fat) is highlighted and compressed
- The neck appears shorter

This is not opinion. It is the predictable result of perspective distortion: a wide-angle or standard lens placed below the subject elongates nearer features relative to farther ones.

The fix costs nothing: hold the phone at eye level or above.

---

## Above vs. at eye level

Slightly above (5-10 cm): the classic flattering portrait position. Jawline reads sharper, neck reads longer.

Directly above (bird&rsquo;s eye): the face looks round and the body disappears. Useful for some contexts, not for progress tracking or professional photos.

For weekly progress photos, consistent is more important than perfect. Pick a height, mark it on the wall, and use it every week.

# Framing and turning

---

## Three-quarter vs. straight-on

Straight-on framing shows the full width of your face and is the standard for passport photos and driver&rsquo;s licenses. It works well if your face is narrower or has clear cheekbone definition.

Three-quarter framing (body and face turned 45 degrees from the camera) does three things:
- Narrows a wider face by showing it at an angle
- Shows the jaw and cheekbone in a single view
- Creates depth by making near features appear larger than far ones

For most faces, three-quarter framing is more flattering than straight-on. The difference is most visible in photos taken at medium to close range.

---

## Applying the three-quarter turn

The turn is subtle. Do not turn your body 90 degrees and look back at the camera over your shoulder. That looks posed.

Instead:
- Turn your body 30-45 degrees away from the camera
- Turn your head back toward the lens until your nose is pointing roughly at the camera
- Keep your chin slightly forward and down (not tucked hard, just not pulled back)

This is the &ldquo;posing without posing&rdquo; principle from Mango Street (2020): the adjustments are there, but they should not read as effortful.

# Lighting

---

## Natural light: why it works

Soft natural light from a window or open doorway is diffused by the atmosphere and by the window glass. It wraps around the face and fills shadows rather than creating them.

The rules:
- Face toward the light source, not away from it
- 45-degree angle to the source is the classic three-quarter portrait lighting
- Direct overhead natural light (noon outside) is harsh; move to shade or face a wall that reflects light back

Natural light is free and available. If you have access to a north-facing window or open shade, you already have a better setup than a ring light.

---

## Ring lights: what they actually do

A ring light placed directly in front of the face produces flat, even illumination with catch-lights visible as rings in the eyes.

What it does well: reduces blemishes and shadows by eliminating contrast.

What it does poorly: flattens facial structure. The same shadows that can seem unflattering in harsh light are what give the face depth and make features readable. A face lit entirely evenly looks two-dimensional.

Ring lights became popular for video calls, not for photography. For weekly progress photos, a window is better.

---

## Avoid harsh overhead light

Overhead-only lighting (typical indoor ceiling lights at night) creates:
- Strong under-eye shadows
- Deep nose shadow pointing downward
- Harsh highlight on the forehead and nose tip
- Under-chin shadow that emphasizes submental fullness

If you can only shoot at night with ceiling lights: angle yourself under the light so it is slightly in front of you rather than directly above. Or add a lamp at face level.

# Micro-adjustments

---

## Posing without posing

The goal is to look like yourself at your best, not to look posed. Posed photos read as posed.

Three adjustments that do not look like adjustments:
1. Push your chin forward and slightly down. This tightens the submental area and defines the jaw. It should feel slightly exaggerated; it reads as natural.
2. Roll your shoulders back and down before the photo is taken. Then let them relax. They land in a better position than your default.
3. Turn your body slightly away from the camera and look back into the lens. This creates breadth across the shoulders and draws the viewer&rsquo;s eye toward the face.

Practice these in a mirror. They become automatic within a few sessions.

---

## Updating your weekly photos

Week 2 established your baseline. Now is the first time you apply better technique to the same format.

Keep these constants from week 2:
- Same location, same background
- Same clothing (or same state: shirt or no shirt)
- Same distance from the camera

Add from this week:
- Camera at eye level (mark the wall if needed)
- Soft frontal light (face the window)
- Three-quarter turn for one of the three shots

Consistency enables comparison. Better technique enables the photo to show what is actually there.

---

## Take-aways

1. Lens below eye level is the single most common photo mistake. The fix costs nothing: hold the phone at eye level or higher.
2. Three-quarter framing narrows a wider face, shows the jaw, and creates depth. Straight-on works for narrower faces with strong definition.
3. Soft natural window light beats ring light for progress photos: it preserves facial structure while filling harsh shadows.
4. Three micro-adjustments: chin forward and down, shoulders back before relaxing, slight body turn with head toward the lens.
5. Consistent setup enables comparison. Better technique enables accurate tracking.
