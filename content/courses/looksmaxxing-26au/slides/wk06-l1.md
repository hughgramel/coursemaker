# Plaque biofilm: the enemy you cannot see

---

## What plaque biofilm is

Dental plaque is not just food residue. It is a structured biofilm.

- Bacteria colonize the tooth surface within hours of cleaning
- They secrete a polysaccharide matrix that anchors them in place
- The biofilm protects bacteria from saliva and low-concentration antimicrobials
- Left undisturbed for 48-72 hours, biofilm begins to mineralize into calculus (tartar)
- Calculus cannot be removed by brushing; it requires professional scaling

**Mechanical disruption every 12-24 hours is the only reliable way to prevent buildup.**

---

## Why brushing angle matters

The ADA recommends a 45-degree angle to the gumline for one specific reason.

- At 45 degrees, bristle tips reach into the gingival sulcus (the small pocket between tooth and gum)
- This is where supragingival plaque accumulates and where gingivitis starts
- Horizontal scrubbing misses the sulcus and can abrade enamel over years
- Short tooth-wide back-and-forth strokes, not long sweeps

> "Position your toothbrush at a 45-degree angle to the gums, and move it back and forth in short (tooth-wide) strokes."
>
> American Dental Association (MouthHealthy.org, 2023)

---

## The two-minute rule

Most people brush for 45 seconds. Two minutes is the ADA recommendation.

- Four quadrants: 30 seconds each
- Outer surfaces, inner surfaces, chewing surfaces
- Soft bristles only: medium and hard bristles increase enamel abrasion risk
- Replace the brush when bristles splay, or every 3-4 months
- Powered toothbrushes are not required but can improve technique consistency for some users

(ADA MouthHealthy.org, 2023)

# The flossing evidence: what we actually know

---

## What the Cochrane review found

The 2019 Cochrane systematic review by Worthington et al. is the most rigorous available synthesis on interdental cleaning.

Main findings:

- Flossing plus brushing may reduce gingivitis and plaque more than brushing alone
- Evidence is rated **low to very low certainty**
- Most trials had high risk of bias; results were inconsistent across studies
- No evidence that interdental cleaning reduces severe periodontitis or interproximal caries

Low certainty does not mean the effect is absent. It means the existing trials are not well-designed enough to be confident.

(Worthington et al., Cochrane, 2019)

---

## Interdental brushes vs floss

The same Cochrane review found a notable secondary result.

- Interdental brushes may be more effective than floss at reducing gingivitis
- Interdental brushes clean more of the tooth surface in proximal contacts
- They are easier to use correctly than floss for most people
- Floss remains appropriate where the contact is too tight for an interdental brush

**Practical takeaway:** if you find interdental brushes easier to use consistently, the evidence supports using them. Compliance matters more than the tool.

(Worthington et al., Cochrane, 2019)

# Tongue cleaning and halitosis

---

## Where bad breath actually comes from

Halitosis has two primary oral sources.

1. **Dorsal tongue surface:** the largest reservoir of volatile sulfur compounds (VSCs) in the mouth. Bacteria in tongue coating metabolize protein debris and produce hydrogen sulfide and methyl mercaptan.
2. **Subgingival plaque:** bacteria in gum pockets produce VSCs continuously; untreated gingivitis significantly worsens breath.

Minor contributors: dry mouth (reduced saliva flow), diet (onion, garlic metabolites persist systemically), and systemic conditions (acid reflux, nasal drip).

Mouthwash masks odor for 30-60 minutes; it does not address the biofilm source.

---

## Tongue scraping: mechanism and method

A tongue scraper is more effective than brushing the tongue surface.

- Brushing the tongue dislodges the surface layer but disperses bacteria laterally
- A scraper removes the entire coated layer in 3-5 strokes from back to front
- The gag reflex is less likely because the scraper moves toward the tip, not toward the throat
- Once daily, integrated into the bedtime routine, is sufficient for most people

Note: tongue cleaning reduces but does not eliminate VSCs. If halitosis persists after consistent oral hygiene, see a dentist to rule out periodontal disease.

---

## Habit stacking your oral routine

Apply the habit-stacking framework from week 2: anchor new steps to existing ones.

Morning anchor (after waking):
- Brush 2 min (45-degree angle, short strokes) with fluoride toothpaste
- Tongue scrape: 3-5 strokes
- Do not rinse immediately; residual fluoride continues working

Bedtime anchor (last thing before sleep):
- Interdental cleaning first (floss or interdental brush): this loosens biofilm
- Brush 2 min: removes what interdental cleaning loosened
- Tongue scrape if morning-only felt insufficient

---

## Take-aways

1. Plaque biofilm is a structured bacterial matrix that reforms within hours; mechanical disruption twice daily is the only way to prevent it from mineralizing.
2. The ADA recommends a 45-degree angle to the gumline and short tooth-wide strokes; this technique targets the gingival sulcus where gingivitis begins.
3. The Cochrane 2019 review found low-to-very-low certainty evidence that interdental cleaning reduces gingivitis; this is a reason to be honest about the evidence, not a reason to skip it.
4. Interdental brushes may outperform floss for gingivitis reduction; use whichever you will do consistently.
5. The tongue dorsum is the primary source of halitosis; a scraper removes the coated layer more effectively than a toothbrush.

---

> "Use of floss or interdental brushes in addition to toothbrushing may reduce gingivitis or plaque, or both, more than toothbrushing alone, though evidence is low-to-very-low certainty. Interdental brushes may be more effective than floss."

**Worthington HV, MacDonald L, Poklepovic Pericic T, et al.**
