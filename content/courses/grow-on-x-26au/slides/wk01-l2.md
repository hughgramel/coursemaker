# Part 1: the niche

---

## Niche is a positioning decision, not a topic

- &ldquo;Personal finance&rdquo; = a topic. Anyone can claim it.
- &ldquo;Debt payoff strategies for nurses in their first five years&rdquo; = a niche
- The reader immediately knows: is this for me?
- Narrow niches convert profile visits to follows at a higher rate

---

## Why narrow wins early

- Graham (2013): Facebook launched Harvard-only. Intensity within a small group produces word-of-mouth.
- A creator unmistakably useful to one person grows faster among that person&rsquo;s peers
- Being vaguely interesting to everyone = being precisely relevant to no one
- You can broaden later. You cannot build momentum without a core.

---

## The Personal Monopoly

- Perell (2019): the intersection of your skills, interests, and perspective that no one else occupies the same way
- Not about being the only expert. About being the only *you* serving *this* reader.
- Niche clarity comes from action, not contemplation: you find your niche by writing, not by planning

---

## Finding the niche: Welsh&rsquo;s three steps

1. Find a market where people already spend money on the problem
2. Immerse in the conversations: forums, comment sections, X replies
3. Find where your skills intersect with that demand

The intersection is your niche. &ldquo;The best ideas don&rsquo;t come from brainstorming in isolation.&rdquo; (Welsh, 2024)

# Part 2: voice

---

## The who/what/how triangle

Three corners. All three must be clear.

- **Who you serve:** a specific person with a specific problem
- **What you say:** the claims and frameworks you consistently bring
- **How you sound:** tone, rhythm, humor, how you handle disagreement

Most accounts have one strong corner and two weak ones.

---

## Diagnosing the weak corner

<!-- _class: two-col -->

**Weak &ldquo;who&rdquo;**
- Clear opinions, no specific reader
- Content talks to everyone
- Connects with no one
- Fix: write for one person, not a demographic

**Weak &ldquo;how&rdquo;**
- Clear who and what
- Voice is generic
- Interchangeable with a dozen accounts in the niche
- Fix: find the one stylistic choice that is unmistakably you

# Part 3: bio craft

---

## The bio is the decision page

- A profile visitor has already expressed interest: they clicked
- You have roughly three seconds and 160 characters
- Most bios treat this as a resume. Resumes describe the past.
- A converting bio makes a promise about the future.

---

## Four elements that convert

1. **Name/identity:** who you help and what you help them do (not your job title)
2. **Headline:** the specific outcome or transformation you deliver
3. **Proof:** one piece of evidence the headline is credible
4. **CTA:** one next step (newsletter, pinned thread, free resource)

Missing any one element leaks follows you already earned.

---

## A bio rewrite, live

**Before:**
```
Software engineer at a startup. I write about tech,
productivity, and life. Occasional runner. Views are my own.
```

**After:**
```
I help new engineers survive (and enjoy) their first startup year.
3 years at Series A-C companies. Writing what I wish I&rsquo;d known.
Free weekly guide: [link]
```

Identity. Headline. Proof. CTA.

---

## Profile photo and header

- Photo: face clearly visible, high contrast. People follow people.
- Header: one clear signal. A single line of text, a visual that implies your topic, or social proof.
- Header should reinforce the bio, not contradict or ignore it.
- Both are visible before the visitor reads a word.

## Take-aways

1. A niche is a positioning decision. Narrow beats broad for early growth because it produces the word-of-mouth that broad never does.
2. The who/what/how triangle diagnoses positioning gaps. Find the weakest corner and fix it first.
3. A bio that converts has four elements: identity, headline, proof, CTA. Treat it as a landing page, not a resume.
4. Photo and header signal before the visitor reads. Make them consistent with the bio.
