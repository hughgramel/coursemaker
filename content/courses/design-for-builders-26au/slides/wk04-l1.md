# Two color models worth knowing

---

## HSL: the model you already use

Three channels: Hue (0-360°), Saturation (0-100%), Lightness (0-100%).

- Hue 0 = red, 120 = green, 240 = blue
- Saturation 0 = gray at any hue, 100 = most vivid
- Lightness 0 = always black, 100 = always white

CSS: `hsl(216, 80%, 52%)`, readable, editable, the default in design tools.

---

## The HSL problem: equal steps, unequal brightness

Set three colors to the same HSL lightness, say, 50%:

- Yellow at hsl(60, 100%, 50%)
- Blue at hsl(240, 100%, 50%)
- Red at hsl(0, 100%, 50%)

Place them side by side. Yellow looks dramatically lighter than blue.

**HSL lightness is not perceptual brightness.** (Koopersmith & Miner, Stripe, 2019)

---

## Why this matters for ramps

If you generate a ramp by incrementing HSL lightness by 10% per step, some steps will look far apart and others will look almost identical.

The ramp will look uneven even though the numbers are perfectly regular.

**Fix: adjust individual steps by eye, or switch to OKLCH.**

---

## OKLCH: perceptual uniformity built in

Three channels: Lightness (L), Chroma (C), Hue (H).

- L is calibrated to human vision: equal steps in L look equal
- C is chroma (equivalent to saturation, but perceptually uniform)
- H is hue angle, similar to HSL hue

CSS: `oklch(0.6 0.12 240)`, available in Figma and Chrome DevTools.

**Ramps built in OKLCH need less hand-tuning than ramps built in HSL.**

---

<!-- _class: two-col -->

# HSL vs OKLCH: which to use

**HSL**

- Available in all tools today
- Familiar from CSS
- Manual step adjustment needed
- Fine for hand-tuned ramps

**OKLCH**

- Available in Figma (2024) and modern browsers
- Better for automated ramp generation
- Less manual correction needed
- Future-proof (CSS Color Level 4)

Both produce good results if you check visually. The model is a starting point.

# Why pure black and pure white are wrong

---

## Pure black: no room to move

`#000000` is the heaviest foreground possible. It creates harsh halation against light backgrounds.

More practically: if your darkest foreground is already `#000`, you have no darker state for pressed, active, or disabled variants.

**The darkest value in a good neutral ramp is typically around 6-12% HSL lightness, not 0%.**

---

## Pure white: glare and no room to layer

`#ffffff` on screens (especially OLED) creates glare in low light.

More practically: if your lightest surface is `#fff`, a card on that surface has nowhere lighter to go. Card elevation requires a subtly different background.

**The lightest value in a good neutral ramp is typically around 96-98% HSL lightness, not 100%.**

---

## Tinting your neutral ramp

A neutral ramp tinted toward your brand hue produces subtle harmony.

```
Pure gray:  hsl(  0, 0%, 97%)  -- clinical, flat
Cool gray:  hsl(220, 9%, 97%)  -- slightly blue
Warm gray:  hsl( 30, 6%, 97%)  -- slightly cream
```

Match the hue of your neutral to your accent color.
Low saturation (4-10%) is enough. More than 12% stops reading as neutral.

(Wathan & Schoger, Refactoring UI, 2019)

# Accent vs neutral: the two-ramp structure

---

## What a ramp is

A ramp is 9 shades of the same color family, labeled 100 (lightest) through 900 (darkest), with an optional 50 for near-white surfaces.

Each step has a clear purpose:

```
100-200:  tinted surfaces, badge backgrounds
300-400:  borders, dividers, disabled states
500:      base color (closest to the brand)
600-700:  text on light surfaces, interactive elements
800-900:  high-emphasis foreground on light surfaces
```

---

## The accent ramp

Your accent is your brand color, the hue you use for buttons, links, and primary interactive elements.

Build 9 steps around your base:
- Steps 100-200: pastel backgrounds for alerts, badges
- Step 500: brand color
- Steps 600-700: interactive elements on light surfaces (usually darker than 500 for contrast)

**Saturated colors need more correction than neutrals** because perceptual brightness varies more steeply at high saturation.

---

## The neutral ramp

Neutrals carry everything else: text, backgrounds, borders, dividers.

One neutral ramp is usually enough for a product. Use a single hue throughout (slightly blue, slightly warm, or pure) and keep saturation low and consistent.

```
Neutral/50:  hsl(220,  9%, 97%)  -- page background
Neutral/500: hsl(220,  9%, 50%)  -- mid-gray
Neutral/900: hsl(220,  9%, 11%)  -- primary foreground
```

The whole ramp is 9 values. That is enough for any product.

---

## Building the ramp: the three-step process

Wathan & Schoger (Refactoring UI, 2019):

1. **Choose the base.** Usually step 500, but adjust if the brand color is very dark or light.
2. **Define the endpoints.** Step 100 = lightest useful value. Step 900 = darkest useful value. Neither is `#fff` or `#000`.
3. **Fill the middle.** Work outward from the base. Adjust each stop until the visual progression looks even.

**Trust your eyes over arithmetic.**

---

## Take-aways

1. Use HSL for manual ramp tuning; use OKLCH if you want automated steps to look perceptually even.
2. Pure black and pure white belong in almost no production palette. Near-extremes give you room for layering and interactive states.
3. Every product palette needs two ramps: a neutral (for text, surfaces, borders) and an accent (for interactive elements and brand color).
4. A ramp generator is a first draft. Check every stop visually and adjust until the progression looks even, not just numerically regular.
