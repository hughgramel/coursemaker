# What Gestalt is

---

## Gestalt: the brain completes the picture

Gestalt psychology (early 20th century, Wertheimer, Kohler, Koffka) describes how the brain perceives whole patterns from partial information.

- The brain does not see a collection of pixels; it sees objects and groups
- Grouping happens automatically, before conscious attention
- The designer who understands Gestalt can predict how any layout will be perceived
- The designer who ignores it produces interfaces that look fine individually but feel wrong in context

NN/G (2020): "Gestalt principles explain why users see a group of elements as a single unit, even when they aren't connected."

---

## Why Gestalt matters for builders

You do not control whether your users apply Gestalt. They already are.

- Every layout you ship is being perceived through proximity, similarity, and figure-ground
- If the groupings your layout produces do not match your information architecture, users will be confused, and they will not be able to tell you why
- Gestalt violations are "invisible bugs": no crash, no error, just a layout that feels harder to use than it should be

The goal of this lecture: make those bugs visible so you can fix them deliberately.

# Proximity

---

## Proximity: the strongest grouping signal

Elements placed close together are perceived as one group. No shared color, shape, or style is needed.

- Proximity works without any other visual property
- It is the first grouping signal the eye resolves, before color or similarity
- It explains why whitespace is not decoration: whitespace creates the gaps that define groups
- A layout with uniform spacing throughout has no groups, it reads as one block of content

NN/G Gestalt video (2020): proximity is the principle designers apply most often and most incorrectly, usually by not applying it at all.

---

## Proximity: the inner/outer gap rule

Every section in your layout has two kinds of gaps:

- **Inner gap:** between items that belong to the same group (e.g., a label and its input field)
- **Outer gap:** between groups (e.g., the shipping section and the billing section)

The outer gap must be visibly larger than the inner gap. "Visibly" means at a glance, not with a ruler.

Recommended ratios (8-point grid):
- Inner gap: 4-8px (one or two grid units)
- Outer gap: 24-48px (three to six grid units)
- Section gap: 48-80px

---

## Proximity: a broken example

A form with six fields. The developer used 16px between every field throughout.

User perception: six unrelated fields that happen to be stacked.

Fix: group the three address fields with 8px between them, then place 32px between the address group and the three payment fields.

User perception after fix: two tasks, address, then payment, without adding a single heading or dividing line.

The content did not change. The grouping changed.

# Similarity

---

## Similarity: shared properties imply shared category

Elements that look alike are perceived as belonging together, even when they are not adjacent.

- Similarity works across color, size, shape, and visual style
- It is how navigation bars work: every nav item has the same weight, size, and padding, so they read as a single group even though each leads somewhere different
- It is how data tables work: every row has the same height and font, so the rows read as entries in one set
- It explains why one off-brand element in a list reads as broken: the similarity signal is disrupted

---

## Similarity in component design

When you build a component set, you are encoding similarity rules.

- All primary buttons: same background color, same weight, same radius
- All secondary buttons: same border color, same weight, same radius
- Primary and secondary must differ on at least two properties to read as different categories (not just different states)

If your secondary button is identical to your primary button but lighter, the user may read them as the same button in different states, not as different actions.

Malewicz (2022): similarity is the principle that makes design systems work. Violating it costs user trust faster than any other single error.

---

## Similarity and visual hierarchy together

Similarity can reinforce hierarchy or undermine it.

- Reinforcing: all H2 headings share size and weight; all H3 headings share a different size and weight. Similarity within levels; contrast between levels.
- Undermining: three buttons in a row, all styled identically. Which one is primary? Similarity has erased the hierarchy the interaction design requires.

Rule: use similarity to group elements of the same *type*. Use contrast to distinguish elements of different *importance*.

# Closure and continuity

---

## Closure: the eye completes implied shapes

The brain perceives a complete shape even when part of it is missing, as long as enough information is present to infer the rest.

- A circle with a gap reads as a circle, not as an arc, if the gap is small relative to the circumference
- The FedEx logo arrow is formed entirely by negative space between two letters
- Progress indicators use incomplete circles to communicate partial completion without the user consciously reasoning about it

Closure allows designers to suggest shapes without drawing them fully, reducing visual noise while maintaining communication.

---

## Continuity: the eye follows implied paths

Continuity says the brain prefers to follow a smooth path rather than a sharp turn.

- A dotted line implies the path continues even where no line exists
- Elements aligned on a horizontal baseline read as a row even with large gaps between them
- A sequence of numbered steps reads as a path even when the steps are separated by large blocks of content
- Navigation breadcrumbs rely on continuity: the separators imply a path back

Both closure and continuity matter in UI because they let you imply structure without drawing every line of it explicitly.

---

## Using closure in icon design

Closure is why minimal icons work.

- A circle with a slash does not need to be fully drawn to read as "prohibited"
- A magnifying glass handle does not need to meet the circle to be recognized as "search"
- The eye fills the gap using context and prior exposure

The risk: too much gap breaks closure. The eye can only fill what it can infer. If your icon removes too many strokes, it stops reading as the intended shape and starts reading as abstract noise.

Test: show the icon to someone with no context at 24px. Can they name what it represents in under two seconds?

# Figure-ground

---

## Figure-ground: object versus canvas

Every visual composition separates into a figure (the shape being perceived) and a ground (the background it sits against).

- The figure is the object of attention
- The ground is what the figure sits on
- The brain always makes this separation, you cannot prevent it
- What you can control is which shape reads as figure and which reads as ground

Gordon (2020): figure-ground is the basis of every modal, tooltip, dropdown, and overlay pattern in UI.

---

## Figure-ground in product interfaces

<!-- _class: two-col -->

**Figure-ground working**

A white card on a gray page background reads as a figure. The content inside the card reads as belonging to that figure. The modal overlay reads as a figure above the dimmed page.

The eye knows where to look because the figure/ground separation is unambiguous.

**Figure-ground broken**

A white card on a white page. The card has a 1px border that disappears at small sizes. The user cannot tell where the card ends and the page begins.

The eye cannot resolve the figure because there is no ground contrast.

---

## Reversible figure-ground

Some compositions are intentionally reversible: both shapes read as figure depending on attention.

- The Rubin vase: two faces or a vase, depending on which shape you assign as ground
- The FedEx arrow: reads as a logo first, then the arrow appears when you see the negative space as figure

In product design, accidental reversibility is a problem. If your content card reads as both card and page, the user has no anchor.

Use sufficient ground contrast: background color, shadow, or border that makes the boundary unambiguous.

# Gestalt as a debugging tool

---

## When a layout feels cluttered, look for Gestalt violations

Most "cluttered" layouts break exactly one of the four principles.

- **Proximity violation:** inner and outer gaps are equal, so nothing groups
- **Similarity violation:** too many visual styles used for the same type of element
- **Closure violation:** icons or shapes with too many strokes removed, or implied paths that the eye cannot follow
- **Figure-ground violation:** insufficient contrast between the content surface and the page, or between the interactive element and its background

Before adding more visual decoration to fix a cluttered layout, audit it for these four violations first.

---

## Applying Gestalt to fix a real layout

Hypothetical screen: a dashboard card with a title, a value, a sparkline, a label, and a small help icon. The user says it "feels busy."

Audit:
1. **Proximity:** title and value are 8px apart. Value and sparkline are also 8px apart. They read as three separate things, not as one metric. Fix: collapse title-to-value gap to 4px; add 20px before the sparkline.
2. **Similarity:** the help icon uses a different icon weight than the rest of the product. Fix: match weight to the system icon style.
3. **Figure-ground:** the card has no drop shadow and the same background as the page. Fix: add 1px border at 10% opacity or a 2px shadow.

No new information was added. The card now reads as one thing.

---

## Take-aways

1. Gestalt grouping happens automatically. You are not deciding whether it happens; you are deciding whether it works for you or against you.
2. Proximity is the strongest grouping signal. Inconsistent spacing is the single most common source of perceived clutter in builder-made interfaces.
3. Similarity encodes category. When similar elements have different treatments, the user assumes the difference is meaningful.
4. Closure and continuity let you imply structure without drawing it, reducing visual noise.
5. Figure-ground separation is the foundation of every overlay, modal, and card pattern in product UI.
